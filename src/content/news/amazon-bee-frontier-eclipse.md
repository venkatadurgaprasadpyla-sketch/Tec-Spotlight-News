You leave a long meeting, and a short summary and a to-do list are already waiting on your phone. That is the promise of the Amazon Bee Frontier, a small wristband that went on sale on Thursday. Amazon also announced a phone attachment, Bee Eclipse, which arrives later. Both listen to your conversations and turn them into notes.

These are the first new devices since Amazon bought Bee, a San Francisco startup, in July 2025. This suggests that AI which listens all day is becoming a cheap, mass-market product, sold by the company that also runs one of the world's biggest online shops.

## Amazon Bee Frontier costs US$75, with a phone attachment and a monthly plan

**Bee Frontier** costs US$74.99 and is available now on Amazon.com (checked on 15 October 2026), up from about US$50 for the first Bee, CNBC reports. You wear it on the wrist or clip it to your clothes. It has two microphones, IP67 water resistance (sealed against dust and a quick dip) and, Amazon says, up to seven days of battery. A green light shows when it is on, and it can switch its microphones off after a set time of silence.

**Bee Eclipse** starts at US$99.99 (checked on 15 October 2026) and "will be available soon", with no date given. It is a thin, credit-card-sized slab that sticks magnetically to the back of a phone. Amazon promises more than 21 days of battery. When switched on, Bee says it processes "both sides of every phone call." Amazon says users are prompted to get permission from the other people on the call each time a recording starts.

So, does the Bee AI wearable require a subscription? Not to work. Every device includes free summaries, recaps and 14 days of memory. Bee Pro, at US$11.99 a month (checked on 15 October 2026), adds unlimited summaries, suggested to-dos, links to Gmail and Google Calendar, and connections to Alexa+ and Amazon.com. New customers get 30 days of Pro free.

## Amazon's privacy promise meets a shopping link

In Amazon's words: "No audio is ever stored. Audio is processed into insights in real time and deleted after processing." Transcripts, it says, are encrypted with keys that only your device creates. The work happens in a locked-down Amazon cloud system called Bee Private Compute. Amazon also says this system was reviewed by the security firm Trail of Bits.

But Bee Pro can now link to Amazon.com and "surface items you mention" as product suggestions. That link is opt-in. Still, things you say can shape what Amazon shows you. Amazon has not explained in detail how that fits with its promise that "no one, including Amazon, can access your data". We found no independent privacy expert commenting on the new devices yet.

There is also everyone else in the room. As CNBC notes, "When turned on, Bee devices are always recording," unlike Echo speakers, which wait for a wake word. In January, the tech site BGR raised concerns about recording people nearby without asking and pointed to Amazon's past settlements with US regulators over Alexa and Ring data.

## If you live outside the US, or simply sit near a wearer

For now, Bee is a US product, sold on Amazon.com in US dollars. Amazon has not said when, or whether, it will sell Bee devices anywhere else.

Still, cheap [listening devices](/news/category/phones-gadgets/) are likely to become common in meetings, classrooms and cafes everywhere. If you use one, tell people when it is on, because a small light on a wrist is easy to miss. Before using Eclipse on calls, check your local recording laws, which differ between countries, and even between US states. Keep the Amazon.com link off if you don't want shopping suggestions, and delete old transcripts. Amazon says you can delete them at any time.

## Is Bee AI legit? Useful help, with trust still to be earned

On balance, the Amazon Bee Frontier looks like a thoughtful product on paper. It costs less than rivals such as the US$159 Plaud Note, TechCrunch notes. It could help students in lectures, carers keeping track of care plans, and anyone who forgets what was agreed. The on-light, the auto-off timer and the prompt to ask permission before recording a call are sensible safeguards.

The harder parts are trust and cost: a year of Bee Pro costs about US$144, almost twice the wristband. On privacy, the claims are Amazon's own, and the shopping link sits uneasily beside them. The people around a wearer never agreed to a microphone. The likelier outcome is that Bee wins fans who value the help, while independent checks decide how far others trust it.

Watch for an Eclipse release date, any launch outside the US, and the first outside review of Bee's privacy claims.
