Most of us have signed a phone contract or a rental lease and felt the weight of the small print. It is a payment you have promised to make for years, whatever happens. Meta has just shown the world its own small print, and it runs to roughly US$825 billion.

The numbers sit in the quarterly report Meta filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (the SEC, America's market regulator) on 22 October. Read together, Meta AI commitments, mostly for data centres, cloud computing and energy, grew by about US$180 billion in a single quarter. On the same day, the cost of insuring Meta's debt reached a record. The money is not spent yet, but much of it is promised.

## Three lines in the filing that add up to US$825 billion

Meta's filing does not print one total. The figure of about US$825 billion is Bloomberg's sum of three items, and our own check of the filing matches it:

- **US$454.52 billion of contracts Meta can't cancel.** Most are for cloud computing capacity bought from other companies, servers and network gear, energy supply for data centres, and hardware for Reality Labs, its virtual reality unit. About US$45.85 billion falls due in the rest of 2026 and US$121.41 billion in 2027.
- **US$355.73 billion of leases that have not started yet.** These cover data centres, energy infrastructure, shared server space and network equipment. They begin between now and 2036 and run for up to 30 years, so they are not yet on Meta's balance sheet.
- **Up to US$14.72 billion of cloud capacity Meta may have to buy** over five years. This amount can shrink if the cloud provider sells that capacity to someone else.

That makes US$824.97 billion. On the same three items, Meta's June filing added up to about US$643 billion. Meta said then that it had signed about US$68 billion more in data-centre leases in July. Bloomberg had put the July total at almost US$700 billion.

## Why Meta AI commitments worried lenders more than shareholders

Meta's shares fell on 22 October, though reports differ on by how much. The clearer signal came from lenders.

Credit default swaps are a kind of insurance against a company failing to repay its debt. According to ICE Data Services figures reported by Bloomberg and TNW, the five-year swaps on Meta's debt rose to about 119 basis points (about US$1.19 a year to protect every US$100 of debt), their highest intraday level since they began trading. Meta's bonds also weakened against US government bonds, and the same insurance cost more for Oracle, Microsoft and Broadcom.

Investors already knew Meta was spending heavily. As we reported in our look at [Meta's third-quarter results](/news/meta-q3-2026-earnings-class-c-shares/), heavy AI spending already turned free cash flow negative, at about minus US$2.1 billion. Meta expects capital spending of US$135 billion to US$145 billion in 2026. The filing adds the bills that come after this year.

## Little changes for app users, but shareholders face a harder question

For the 3.64 billion people who used Meta's apps on an average day in September, by Meta's count, nothing changes this week. These commitments pay for the computing behind AI features in those apps, and for Meta's bet that AI will keep ads working and its products growing.

If you hold Meta shares, directly or through a retirement or index fund, the question is different: can the business earn enough to cover these bills? The filing also shows US$10.80 billion of cash set aside in escrow for some multi-year infrastructure deals, not due for release until 2028 to 2030.

Meta data center leases that run for up to 30 years tie up money for decades. A simple step is to check your fund's list of top holdings, which most funds publish online, to see how much of your money rides on this bet.

## A sound business carrying a long promise

There is real strength here. Meta's revenue rose 26% last quarter, and its core ads business pays for much of this build-out. In practice, long contracts also lock in scarce power and computing at a time when everyone wants them.

The risk is in the length. Leases of up to 30 years and contracts that can't be cancelled assume that demand for AI computing will hold. If AI earns less than hoped, or chips become cheaper and better faster than expected, Meta is still on the hook. On balance, this looks like a confident company making a very large bet in public, which is fair. The record cost of insuring its debt suggests lenders now want clearer proof that the bet will pay.

The next test comes with Meta's full-year results, usually published in late January, and its first spending plan for 2027. Watch whether the commitments keep climbing faster than revenue.
