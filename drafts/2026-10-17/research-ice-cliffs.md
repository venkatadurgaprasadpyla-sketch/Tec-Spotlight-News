## 5. The "not simply reassuring" point (must be in the story)

- U-M release: some estimates of **higher, faster sea-level rise, which some scientists had dismissed, "could be more likely than previously thought"**. [2+, official] U-M release; WHOI/ecomagazine summaries carry the same idea.
- Bassis (U-M): high-end rise tied to rapidly breaking cliffs "has been somewhat dismissed" as unlikely; the study suggests it should not be ruled out; we "may see higher rates of sea level rise than some people thought possible" because of large breaks in parts of the ice sheets. [U-M official; exact wording only from search summaries, keep quote short]
- Balance: the same authors say runaway total collapse is not supported. So the honest reading is: **faster bursts, no runaway**. Higher near-term rates are more plausible; the most extreme "doomsday" chain reaction is less supported by what has been seen so far.

## 7. Limits (put these in "the open question")

- Few very tall cliffs to test: only 16 glaciers above ~90 m (Earth.com); conclusions rest on a handful of glaciers. [Earth.com; phys.org/WHOI echo the "few tall cliffs" point]
- Rates cover **months, not decades**. [Earth.com; WHOI]
- The team **picked retreating glaciers on purpose**, so measured rates are likely at the high end. [WHOI-based summaries; Earth.com]
- The past few years may not predict a warmer future (Bassis's "we do not yet know"). [U-M]
- MICI has **never been directly observed** as a full runaway; this is an observational test, not proof either way. [phys.org; WHOI]

## 9. Context: MICI, the debate, IPCC ranges

**What MICI is.** Marine ice cliff instability: if the floating ice shelves that brace a glacier break away, the exposed ice front can be too tall to hold its own weight (above roughly 100 m above water in older models). It collapses, exposing an even taller face inland (West Antarctica's bed slopes downward inland), and the cycle repeats. [phys.org; Antarctic Environments Portal; NCBI/PMC 2021 modelling paper; Earth.com]

**Why it matters.** DeConto and Pollard (Nature, 2016) used MICI to project Antarctica could add **more than 1 metre** of sea-level rise by 2100 under unchecked emissions (and >15 m by 2500). Later work revised that down: a 2019 Nature study (Edwards et al.) found MICI is unlikely to double sea-level rise by 2100, and the DeConto team's own update lowered their estimate. [Nature 2016 abstract page; Carbon Brief; Science News "Collapsing ice cliffs won't make sea level rise double by 2100"] 
- A 2024 Science Advances paper argued the West Antarctic Ice Sheet may not be vulnerable to MICI this century, and Thwaites's cliffs did not retreat inland at MICI speeds in its simulations (Dartmouth release: "highest prediction of sea-level rise unlikely"). [Science Advances ado7794; Dartmouth 2024; phys.org]
- MIT's own 2019 work: a 90 m cliff collapses fast only if its shelf vanishes within hours. [MIT News 2019; phys.org 2019]
- **Do not use** the phys.org line about "a 2026 Nature analysis by Tamsin Edwards" on past cliff collapse: Science News dates a similar finding differently. [conflict, unresolved: year unclear]

**IPCC AR6 (2021) ranges.** Very high emissions (SSP5-8.5): likely **0.63–1.01 m** by 2100 (median ~0.77 m, relative to 1995–2014). Rise approaching **2 m by 2100** (and 5 m by 2150) "cannot be ruled out" because of deeply uncertain ice-sheet processes, including MICI, which IPCC kept **out of its likely ranges** (low confidence). [IPCC AR6 WG1 SPM + Chapter 9; RealClimate summary]
- For balance, the low-emission IPCC range (SSP1-2.6) is about 0.32–0.62 m; this is from my background knowledge of AR6, **not re-confirmed in this session's searches**. Confirm or leave out.

**Link to our Thwaites brief** (`/news/thwaites-glacier-sea-level/`, 15 Oct): a different process (seawater reaching under the ice at the grounding line, Nature Communications, Poinelli et al.), giving up to 30 mm from Thwaites by 2100 under a 1996–2023 climate. This paper is about what happens at the cliff edge after ice breaks. Natural link sentence: "Earlier this week a separate study found seawater reaching further under Thwaites than models assumed." Keep units in mm/cm and the same calm framing (planning signal, not alarm). Note: that brief calls Thwaites the "Doomsday Glacier"; U-M's release also mentions the "doomsday glaciers" label.

## 10. Reader angle

Coastal cities plan sea walls, drainage and flood maps using ranges like the IPCC's. The tail of those ranges is where MICI sits. This study says: bursts of very fast ice loss are real and maybe faster than modelled (so the upper-middle of the range deserves attention), but a non-stop chain reaction has not been seen. A quiet tech angle: a NASA laser satellite measuring cliff faces every few months turned a decade-old modelling argument into something you can measure.
