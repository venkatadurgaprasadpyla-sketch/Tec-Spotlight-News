## Key facts (source per fact)

**The paper**
- Title, journal, date: as above. Received 3 Aug 2026, accepted 26 Aug 2026, published 15 Oct 2026. [1]
- 12 authors, led by Emma Pomeroy (Dept of Archaeology, Cambridge); last author Graeme Barker (McDonald Institute, Cambridge). Teams from the UK (Cambridge, Liverpool John Moores, Canterbury Archaeological Trust, Birkbeck, Helford Geoscience) and Spain (IPHES Tarragona, Universitat Rovira i Virgili, CENIEH Burgos). [1]
- Shanidar A is an "older adult", about 45 years old, estimated from very heavy tooth wear and comparison with other Shanidar Neanderthals. Sex could not be determined. [1] (ABC calls the individual "a man in his 40s", which contradicts the paper; do not repeat. [4])
- Laid down around 75,000 years ago; this date comes from earlier dating of the same burial cluster (Shanidar Z / 4 cluster, Pomeroy et al. 2020), not a new direct date of these bones. [1]
- Remains: a largely complete right hand, two left ribs, a right rib fragment, a left shoulder blade (scapula) and seven teeth. Excavated in 2019 and 2022. [1] (CNN agrees: 2019 and 2022 [3]. Cambridge says "unearthed in 2020" [2]; New Scientist says six teeth [5]. Use the paper: 2019 and 2022, seven teeth.)
- Body was later disturbed by a flash flood after it decayed. [1]
- Shanidar A raises the burial cluster to at least five individuals (four adults, one infant) [1][2], in a space about 1.5 m long, 1 m wide and 1 m deep, behind a large upright rock. [2] Shanidar A is the deepest and earliest in the cluster sequence. [1]

**The wound**
- A near-perfectly circular hole about 15 mm across in the left shoulder blade. [1]
- Round shape fits a wooden spear with a rounded cross-section; a stone-tipped weapon would usually leave an oval, lozenge or angular hole. [1][2]
- Direction: entered from the front of the body, from above and toward the middle, exiting through the shoulder blade. [1] Cambridge adds that it passed through the lung and likely major arteries, causing rapid death, and that bevelling round the hole is typical of an exit wound. [2]
- No healing, plus small bone fragments found in the sediment beneath the hole, point to an injury around the time of death. [2][4]
- The paper calls it "rare evidence of perimortem penetrating trauma among Neanderthals" and says the hole's shape gives "valuable (albeit indirect)" evidence of wooden tools in the Middle Palaeolithic. [1]
- The paper states plainly that whether this was a hunting accident or violence between people "cannot be known". [1]
- No sign of carnivore damage. [1]
- Future work: experiments on how much force it takes to make this kind of hole. [1][3]

**The earlier face injury and care**
- One lower front tooth (incisor) was split lengthways; half is missing and nearby teeth were turned inward. [1][2]
- Hard plaque (calculus) built up over the broken surface, and the tooth kept wearing down, so it stayed in use in the mouth for some time after the blow. [1]
- Cambridge and Pomeroy say this means survival for at least a year, maybe more, likely with help from others (for example soft food). [2] The paper itself says the injury "suggests care from group members". [1] "At least a year" is the researchers' estimate; I could not find an exact method for this figure in the paper excerpt.
- The paper notes the cause of the face blow can't be pinned down (a fall, violence or other) because the jaw and face bones were not found. [1]

**The "earliest" claim (be careful)**
- The paper itself does NOT say "earliest". It says the wound "adds to a small corpus" of such injuries. [1]
- The "may be the earliest known example of lethal interpersonal violence with a wooden spear in the hominin fossil record" line comes from the Cambridge release. [2] CNN attributes it to "the authors". [3] Always write it as "the researchers say it may be".
- Cambridge also says it could be the first evidence for sharpened wooden spears being used in the prehistoric Middle East. [2]
- Context: older violence exists. Sima de los Huesos skulls (Spain) show deliberate lethal violence about 430,000 years ago, weapon type unknown. [3] Another Shanidar Neanderthal (Shanidar 3, about 50,000 years old, roughly 25,000 years younger than Shanidar A) had a rib puncture that fits a stone-tipped weapon better. [1][3] (Shanidar 3 study: Churchill et al. 2009, *Journal of Human Evolution* 57:163-178, cited in the paper's reference list. [1])

**Wooden spears background**
- Schöningen, Germany: wooden spears found in the 1990s, some over two metres long, now dated to around 200,000 years ago. [2] (CNN says 200,000-300,000 years [3]; the paper cites a 2025 revised age of ~200,000. [1])
- Clacton-on-Sea, UK: tip of a wooden spear over 400,000 years old, found 1911. [2]

**Shanidar Cave history (sourced)**
- In the foothills of Iraqi Kurdistan; one of the world's best-known Neanderthal sites. [2]
- Ralph Solecki's digs in the 1950s found ten individuals, including a cluster of four. [2] First excavated 1953. [4]
- 1960 "flower burial" idea (pollen in graves); the current team has since questioned it, suggesting the pollen may have come later via bees. [3]
- Another Shanidar Neanderthal (Shanidar 1) lived long with a withered arm, deafness and partial blindness, so must have been cared for. [2][3] (CNN cites a 2017 PLOS ONE study and says age about 50-60.)
- The Cambridge-led team restarted digging in 2015. [2][3] Shanidar Z, found earlier in the same cluster, got a facial reconstruction in 2024 (CNN link). [3]

## Exact quotes (speaker and role)

From the team:
- Dr Emma Pomeroy, lead author, Department of Archaeology, University of Cambridge (associate professor in the evolution of health, diet and disease per CNN): "Surviving a blow that splits a tooth in this fashion, then continuing to live and eat for a period of at least a year and maybe more, would have required care and support from other Neanderthals." [2][3]
- Pomeroy: "The remains of Shanidar A reflect the dual nature of so much of what we recognise as human. On the one hand, we have evidence of a terribly violent end. On the other, this was clearly an individual who was cared for during their lifetime." [2]
- Pomeroy on the accident option: "Animals such as mammoths may have been hunted up close with thrusting spears, so it's easy to imagine lethal injuries." [2]
- Pomeroy: "The position of the wound and the force required to push it right through the Neanderthal's body bear the hallmarks of an intended kill shot directed towards the heart." [2]
- Pomeroy (to ABC): "[But] we can never be sure because, of course, the wood has not preserved." [4]
- Pomeroy (to ABC): "We have a tendency to romanticise hunter-gatherer or pre-agriculture lifestyles as peaceful and close to the natural world … but it's not that simple." [4]
- Pomeroy (to New Scientist) on the pain and ancient painkillers: "I don't think they would have touched this pain". [5]
- Prof Graeme Barker, leads the Shanidar Cave excavations, fellow of St John's College, Cambridge: "All we can ever know about the end of this Neanderthal's life is that it was violent, and came at the end of a wooden spear." [2]
- Barker: "Evidence of systematic violence is extremely rare in deep prehistory." [2]
- Barker: "It is fascinating to have evidence for both compassion and violence amongst Neanderthals, our closest evolutionary cousins, embodied in this one individual." [2]

Independent experts (not involved):
- Dr Sofia Samper Carro, archaeologist, Australian National University (sceptical): "I think it's a bit of a stretch with the justification they have so far." [4] Also: "It's unusual that you get those circular shapes, but correlating that with the cause of death? I think it's a bit too much." [4] She agrees the round hole is "weird" and not how bones usually break, but says cause of death is very hard to know without the weapon. [4]
- Prof John McNabb, emeritus professor of Palaeolithic archaeology, University of Southampton: "a spear wound through the shoulder while facing the victim" supports deliberate violence; and "Murder? Attack from a rival Neanderthal group? We just do not know." He called the care evidence a "deeply significant discovery in itself." [3]
- Prof Bruce Hardy, anthropology, Kenyon College, Ohio: the evidence "adds to another chapter of both Neanderthal trauma and Neanderthal care"; "The site of Shanidar continues to astound even some 60 years after its initial excavation." [3]
- Dr Palmira Saladié, Catalan Institute of Human Paleoecology and Social Evolution (IPHES), Spain (recently helped find wooden Neanderthal spears at Abric Romaní): "What I find most striking about Shanidar is how it brings together two seemingly opposite sides of human behaviour." [5] Note: IPHES is also the home institute of co-author Lucía López-Polín, so she is outside the team but not from an unrelated institute.

## What is NOT confirmed

- That it was an attack or murder. The paper says accident vs violence "cannot be known". [1] Cambridge: "no way of knowing the context". [2] A close-range hunting accident is possible. [2]
- Who held the spear. Neanderthal-on-Neanderthal is only the "most straightforward interpretation"; modern humans are not ruled out. [2][3]
- That the weapon was wooden. No wood survived; it's inferred from the round hole and comparison with replica spears. [1][4] Outside expert Samper Carro thinks the link to cause of death is a stretch. [4]
- "Earliest" status: the researchers' "may be" claim, from the press release, not the paper. [1][2]
- The 75,000-year date is from the cluster, not a new direct date. [1]
- Sex unknown. [1] Avoid "he/man" (ABC's wording); use "they" or "the Neanderthal".
- Minor conflicts between sources: dig years (2019 and 2022 per paper vs 2020 per Cambridge), teeth count (seven per paper vs six per New Scientist). Follow the paper.

## Reader angle (worldwide)

- It's a 75,000-year-old story about two things we still struggle with: hurting each other and caring for each other. Both show up in one person.
- It pushes back on the old cartoon of Neanderthals as brutes, and also on the romantic idea that early life was peaceful. Pomeroy says both are stereotypes. [4][5]
- Many people alive today outside sub-Saharan Africa carry some Neanderthal DNA from interbreeding [3] (CNN says the two groups interbred; avoid giving a percentage unless sourced).
- How science works: a 15 mm hole, a tooth crusted with plaque and careful CT scans let researchers rebuild a life, but honest scientists still say "may" and "cannot be known". Good lesson in reading science headlines.
- The site is in Iraqi Kurdistan; Iraq's heritage authorities are not named in our sources, so do not invent credits.

## Fair judgment points

Good:
- Peer-reviewed, open-access paper with a large UK-Spain team; anyone can read it. [1]
- Careful physical evidence: near-intact scapula lifted in one piece, bone fragments recovered and fitted back, micro-CT scanning. [1][2]
- The paper is cautious and openly says the cause can't be known. [1]
- Adds to a growing body of care evidence (Shanidar 1; a Neanderthal child with Down syndrome in Spain; Siberian "dentistry"). [3]
- Rare indirect evidence of wooden weapons, which almost never survive. [1]

Weak spots / caveats:
- No weapon found; "wooden spear" rests on hole shape. [1][4]
- Press release headline ("attack", "earliest") is stronger than the paper. [1][2]
- One outside expert (Samper Carro) calls the cause-of-death link "a bit of a stretch". [4]
- Only a few bones survive (no jaw or face), so the face injury story is partly inference. [1]
- Force experiments are still to come. [1][3]

Calm landing: a strong, careful find about care and a likely violent death, but the "murder" framing is a guess; the honest answer to "who and why" is "we don't know yet".
